Observation first · Explanation second

Observation of a chemical reaction

Record what changed before you name a product. Bubbles, a new solid, or a color change can point to a reaction. Each clue needs context.

See worked examples →

For study only. Physical experiments need trained supervision.

How to write it

Separate what you see from what you infer

An observation describes a change you can notice: “the clear mixture turns cloudy” or “bubbles form.” An inference explains a possible cause: “a solid formed” or “a gas was released.” Write the observation first, then support the explanation with the reactants, equation, and conditions.

One clue alone can mislead. Bubbles may come from boiling or trapped air. A color shift may come from dilution or mixing. A solid may be an undissolved starting material. Check whether the change fits the whole reaction before you identify a product.

Supported examples

Four observations, four explanations

Each example connects a specific visual clue to a balanced equation. The virtual lab predicts these outcomes for supported pairs.

01Cloudiness

Carbon dioxide enters limewater

Observation: The clear limewater becomes cloudy or milky. A white solid appears in the mixture.

CO₂(g) + Ca(OH)₂(aq) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)

Explanation: Calcium carbonate is poorly soluble. Its small solid particles scatter light. The cloudiness is a clue to a precipitate, not a measure of its mass.

Passing excess carbon dioxide can change the later appearance. This simple equation describes the initial result only.

02Gas bubbles

Sodium carbonate meets hydrochloric acid

Observation: The mixture fizzes. Bubbles rise and leave the liquid.

Na₂CO₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

Explanation: The acid reacts with carbonate and releases carbon dioxide. The visible fizz is gas leaving the solution. Bubble size and speed depend on amounts and conditions.

Do not name an unknown gas from fizzing alone. A supervised identification test needs more evidence.

03Colored solid

Silver nitrate meets potassium iodide

Observation: A yellow solid forms when the solutions mix.

AgNO₃(aq) + KI(aq) → AgI(s) + KNO₃(aq)

Explanation: Silver and iodide ions form insoluble silver iodide. Potassium and nitrate ions remain dissolved. The yellow color helps describe the solid but does not prove an unknown sample is silver iodide.

04Blue solid

Copper(II) sulfate meets potassium hydroxide

Observation: A blue solid appears in the liquid.

CuSO₄(aq) + 2KOH(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) + K₂SO₄(aq)

Explanation: Copper(II) ions and hydroxide ions form copper(II) hydroxide. The equation explains why two hydroxide ions are needed per copper ion.

Use this pattern

Write a stronger lab note

Start with the starting appearance and the action: “Two clear solutions were mixed.” Add the change without naming a cause: “A yellow solid appeared.” Then give the proposed explanation with the equation and the specific product. If you did not measure heat, pH, gas volume, or time, do not claim a value for it.

This order makes your answer easier to check. It also shows where the evidence ends. A predicted visual cue can support a study answer, but it cannot replace a real observation made under controlled conditions.

Study boundary

A prediction is not a physical result.

OmniLab's results are educational predictions, not verified simulations. They do not model every concentration, temperature, reaction rate, or competing process.

Use your course materials to check important claims. Follow trained supervision and your laboratory's safety rules. Never use this page as a physical procedure.
